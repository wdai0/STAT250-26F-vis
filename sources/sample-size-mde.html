More observations, smaller detectable effects

The null mean stays at zero. Larger samples narrow both sampling distributions. The cutoff moves toward zero to retain a 5% null tail; the alternative moves toward zero to retain 80% power.

The separation of the means shrinks in outcome units, but stays constant in standard-error units. That is why the shaded proportions remain the same.

What “minimum” means here

A smaller effect can still yield a statistically significant result, but with less than 80% probability. An effect at the target can still be missed in 20% of repeated studies.

Alpha and beta belong to different hypothetical population realities; they are not complementary pieces of one curve. For the selected alternative, beta and power do sum to one.

A statistically detectable effect need not be practically important. Increasing sample size reduces random variation; it does not automatically remove bias or establish causality.

Model assumptions. Independent normal observations, unknown mean μ, known population standard deviation σ = 2 outcome units. Test H₀: μ = 0 against Hₐ: μ > 0; reject when the sample mean exceeds c. The sample size counts original independent observations in one sample, not observations per group or simulation repetitions.

Under mean μ, the sample mean is normal with mean μ and standard deviation 2/√n. The constants are z0.95 = 1.6448536269514722 and z0.80 = 0.8416212335729143. At the selected alternative, (c − μ₁)/SE = −z0.80, leaving 20% below the cutoff and 80% above.

To halve the minimum detectable effect, how much data do we need?

Four times as many independent observations, with the same α, target power, and population SD. For example, increasing n from 100 to 400 reduces the minimum detectable effect from about 0.4973 to 0.2486 units.
