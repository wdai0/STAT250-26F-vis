Different realities, different denominators

These two curves show different hypothetical realities. We do not assume both are true at once.

Each curve has total area 100%. Alpha and beta are areas under different curves, so they do not generally sum to one. Beta and power sum to one for the specified alternative.

A Type II error probability requires a specified real effect. Here that mean is 1; the lower curve does not represent every mean greater than zero.

Choose a rule before seeing the data

Here we compare possible rules before conducting a study. A higher cutoff makes rejection harder: fewer false alarms, but more missed effects when the distributions are fixed.

These are long-run probabilities across repeated studies, not probabilities that a particular completed decision is wrong.

Neither error is automatically worse. Their consequences depend on context. At c = 0.5 the two error probabilities happen to be equal; that does not make it the best cutoff.

Model assumptions. Independent normal observations with unknown mean μ and known population standard deviation σ = 2; n = 25 per study. A positive mean represents improvement over zero. Test H₀: μ = 0 against Hₐ: μ > 0. Reject H₀ when the sample mean exceeds c; otherwise do not reject.

Both sampling distributions have standard deviation σ/√n = 0.4. These are distributions of sample means, not individual observations. Areas are calculated over the full distributions, including the tails outside the fixed display range −2 to 3.

α(c) = 1 − Φ(c/0.4)   ·   β(c; μ = 1) = Φ((c − 1)/0.4)   ·   power = 1 − β

Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The counts per 1,000 are approximate expected frequencies, not simulated results. The wide slider range includes deliberately extreme decision rules.

With the distributions fixed, can moving the cutoff reduce both error probabilities?

No. Raising the cutoff reduces α and increases β; lowering it does the reverse. Changing the study design is a different question, outside this exploration.
