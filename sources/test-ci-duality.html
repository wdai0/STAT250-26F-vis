One candidate, then a whole set

A hypothesis test asks whether these data are sufficiently incompatible with a particular candidate mean. A confidence interval collects the candidate means that the matching tests would not reject.

The observed sample mean stays at 5. Each candidate μ₀ supplies a different hypothetical sampling distribution for X̄, the sample mean across repeated studies:

X̄ | μ = μ₀ ∼ Normal(μ₀, 0.5²)
x̄obs = 5   (fixed observation)

Under each candidate, the two-sided p-value counts sample means at least as far from μ₀ as 5 is. The opposite boundary is 2μ₀ − 5. Both tails together have probability p; α = 0.05 is the threshold for the decision.

At μ₀ = 6, the boundaries are 5 and 7, equally distant from 6, and p ≈ 0.0455003. At μ₀ = 5, every possible sample mean is at least zero units from the candidate, so p = 1.

The sweep reveals an analytically determined set. A finite animation does not estimate its endpoints from a grid. Candidates outside the displayed range 3–7 are also rejected in this model.

Why 5% error corresponds to 95% coverage

For any fixed true mean, a newly sampled interval misses that mean exactly when its matching test rejects it. Here the test has a 5% Type I error rate, so the confidence procedure covers that mean in 95% of repeated samples.

This sweep uses one fixed dataset. It illustrates inversion; the 95% coverage statement concerns new datasets, not the fraction of candidate values retained in the sweep.

Interpret the result carefully

“Do not reject” does not prove the candidate true. A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. This realized interval does not assign a 95% probability to the fixed population mean.

The exact correspondence requires matching procedures: the same model, statistic, sidedness, significance level, and boundary convention. A generic bootstrap percentile interval is not automatically the inverse of an arbitrary randomization test.

Sweeping candidate values is test inversion for one parameter using the same data and testing rule. It is not a search across unrelated research questions for a significant result.

A decision changes at an endpoint, while the evidence changes continuously. Values just inside and just outside can have almost identical p-values.

Model. Sixteen independent normal observations with unknown mean μ and known population SD σ = 2; standard error σ/√n = 0.5. The illustrative observed sample mean is 5. No data are simulated or resampled.

Endpoints and precision. Reject for p < 0.05 and retain equality, giving a closed 95% interval. Decisions use its full-precision analytical bounds; the endpoint buttons select those same stored bounds. Printed endpoints are rounded, and near-boundary p-values are explicitly marked approximate. This convention prevents floating-point rounding from contradicting interval membership.

Why does the interval stay fixed when we test a different candidate?

The observed data, standard error, and confidence level have not changed. We are checking another member of the same set of candidate values.
